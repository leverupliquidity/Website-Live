Most cannabis owners think their credit score is why the bank turned them down. It usually isn't. Banks pass on cannabis businesses because of federal risk. You can still get a cannabis business loan. The lenders who say yes look at revenue, time in business and an active state license first, and some will work with credit scores above 500.

## Can you get a loan for a cannabis business?

Yes. Licensed dispensaries, cultivators and processors can get lines of credit, term loans, equipment financing, merchant cash advances and commercial real estate loans. Most of that money doesn't come from a traditional bank. It comes from a small group of lenders that work with cannabis, and each one has its own rules about which files it accepts.

That last part is where owners lose the most time. An application sent to the wrong lender gets declined no matter how strong the business is, and each decline costs you a week you could have spent running the store. The real work is matching your file to a lender that already says yes to files like yours.

## Why do most banks turn down cannabis businesses?

Because adult-use cannabis is still illegal under federal law, and banks answer to federal regulators. About 1,000 banks and credit unions filed cannabis-related reports with FinCEN in fiscal year 2024, roughly 11% of insured institutions, according to the [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/888233.pdf). A report isn't a loan. Many of those institutions offer cannabis businesses limited lending or none.

The rules did move this year, but not far enough to change how banks lend. The federal government moved state-licensed medical marijuana to Schedule III, effective April 28, 2026. Adult-use cannabis stayed in Schedule I, and a decision on broader rescheduling is still pending after a DEA hearing this summer ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/09/high-time-for-a-change-post-dea-hearing-broader-marijuana-rescheduling-questions-remain)). The [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/tort_trial_insurance_practice/resources/tortsource/2026-summer/marijuana-finally-rescheduled-after-55-years) notes that rescheduling alone doesn't change banking. Banks still carry federal risk until Congress passes a cannabis banking law or FinCEN updates its guidance.

That risk shows up in price. In GAO's focus groups of cannabis business owners, seven of eight groups reported loan rates above 15%. For comparison, a Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City survey cited in the same report found median small-business loan rates of about 7.4% to 7.9% in early 2025. Most of that gap comes from the industry's federal status, not from your credit score.

## What do cannabis lenders look at instead of your credit score?

Revenue, time in business and an active state license come first. Lenders want steady deposits that can carry the payment, enough history to trust the numbers, and a license in good standing, because without the license there is no business. Credit still counts, but a score above 500 can be enough for some products.

Here's why each one matters to a lender:

- **Revenue.** Your bank deposits show whether the business can make the payment. Lenders read these more closely than your tax return.
- **Time in business.** Six months of sales history lets a lender see how your cash moves, including the slow weeks.
- **An active license.** If the license lapses, the business can't legally operate, so the lender's money is at risk on day one.
- **Credit.** It shapes which products and terms you're offered. It's rarely the reason a cannabis file is declined.

## Do you qualify? The four minimums

You likely meet the minimums if your business has been operating for at least 6 months, brings in at least $250,000 a year (about $21,000 a month), has an owner with a credit score above 500 and holds an active state cannabis license. Missing one usually means waiting a few months, not giving up for good.

1. At least 6 months in business
2. At least $250,000 in annual revenue, about $21,000 a month
3. A credit score above 500
4. An active state cannabis license

If you've been open less than six months, the honest answer is to wait. Applying early adds declines to your history and doesn't speed anything up. Use the time to keep clean bank statements and make sure your license is current.

## Which cannabis business loan fits what you need?

Match the product to the job. A line of credit covers uneven cash flow. A term loan pays for a one-time project. Equipment financing buys machines, and a merchant cash advance gives fast, short-term cash against future sales. A commercial real estate loan buys or refinances property. Using the wrong one is the most common way owners overpay.

The steps are the same for all three: gather your bank statements and license, check the four minimums, and apply through a lender or broker that works with cannabis. What changes is the product. Dispensaries often need inventory cash, cultivators need equipment between harvests, and processors need both while they wait for retailers to pay.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I qualify for a cannabis business loan?

You likely qualify if you've been in business at least 6 months, earn at least $250,000 a year, have a credit score above 500 and hold an active state cannabis license.

### What are the best cannabis business loans for startups with low credit scores?

If you've been open less than 6 months, most cannabis lenders will say no for now. After 6 months and $250,000 in annual revenue, credit scores above 500 can qualify for some products.

### Which cannabis lenders offer flexible repayment terms?

Payments are weekly or monthly, depending on the product and lender. Typical terms run from 8 months on a merchant cash advance to 7 years on a commercial real estate loan.

### How do you get a business loan for a cannabis farm?

Licensed cultivators use the same four minimums. Equipment financing is a common fit for grows, with an average deal of $70,000 over a typical 4-year term.

### Will checking whether I qualify hurt my credit?

No. The first check is a soft pull, which doesn't affect your credit score.
